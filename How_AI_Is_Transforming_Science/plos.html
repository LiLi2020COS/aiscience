AI Is Reshaping How Science Is Conducted

An empirical analysis of 353,810 PLOS articles received between 2003 and 2025—predominantly in clinical medicine—traces the rise of AI-driven workflows, shifting primary-versus-secondary data provenance, and widening structural ratios across three methodological eras.

SECTION I.1 · TWENTY-THREE YEARS OF PUBLISHED ARTICLES

Longitudinal Shifts in AI Adoption and Data Provenance

Among 353,810 PLOS articles received between 2003 and 2025—predominantly in clinical medicine—the proportion reporting primary data fell from about 66% in the machine-learning era (66.0%) to under 47% in the generative-AI era (46.8%, and 46.6% in incomplete 2025* records). Over the same twenty-three-year span, the share relying on previously collected data rose from below 14% (13.7%) to almost 34% (33.5%, peaking at 33.8% in 2024), while the share identified as AI-driven increased from under 2% (1.6%) to over 11% (11.3%), reaching 15.6% in incomplete 2025* (Fig. 1a–c).

Within the 14,507 articles identified as using AI methods since 2006, methodological composition also transformed: classical machine learning declined from roughly 86%–91% during 2007–2013 to 37.4% in 2025*, overtaken by deep learning (55.4%) and a fast-emerging wave of generative AI (6.5%) and hybrid methods (0.7%). Simultaneously, structural indicators tracking the balance between data reuse and original collection—both the consumer-to-primary-data-contributor ratio (CPDC) and normalized Shannon evenness—climbed steadily after 2012, reaching 0.544 in 2024 (0.504 in 2025*) and 0.843 in 2025*, respectively (Fig. 1d).

Interpretive Caveat · Non-Causal Compositional Signal

These estimates do not mean that AI is displacing primary-data-based research. Changes in journal coverage, article types, research practices, or classification performance could each produce such trends, and a smaller proportion of papers reporting primary data does not necessarily mean less data is being collected, since growth in data-reuse studies can dilute primary-data share without coming at the expense of primary collection. Expanding to a larger corpus improves precision only when underlying classifications are rigorously validated.

SECTION I.2 · METHODOLOGICAL ERAS & MATCHED QUARTERS

Era-Level Shifts and Within-Period AI Association

Aggregating the corpus across three methodological eras—the Machine Learning era (ML: 2003–2014, n = 142,803), the Deep Learning era (DL: 2015–2022, n = 166,917), and the Generative AI era (GenAI*: 2023–2025*, n = 44,090)—highlights the scale of the compositional transition. Era-mean AI adoption expanded nearly sevenfold from 1.6% in ML to 4.3% in DL and 11.3% in GenAI* (Fig. 2a). Over those same three eras, primary producers stepped down from 66.0% to 58.1% and 46.8%, mixotrophs eased from 20.0% to 15.6%, data consumers more than doubled from 13.7% to 33.5%, and articles without empirical data rose from 0.2% to 4.1% (Fig. 2b).

Crucially, the association between AI methods and secondary data consumption persists even when holding calendar time constant. Comparing matched received-date quarters across all three eras (ML: n = 40 quarters; DL: n = 32; GenAI*: n = 11), AI-classified papers exhibited significantly higher quarterly CPDC means than non-AI papers in every period: 1.164 vs. 0.176 in ML, 1.122 vs. 0.280 in DL, and 1.464 vs. 0.453 in GenAI* (all Holm-adjusted p < .001; Fig. 2c).

SECTION I.3 · EMPIRICAL SYNTHESIS

What Is Missing: A Measure of Empirical Contribution

“What is missing, then, is not evidence but a measure. Publication counts record that a paper exists; they do not record whether it added an observation to the world or reanalysed observations already there. Without that distinction, the balance could shift without anyone noticing—and the first to notice might be the end users needing data that was no longer collected.”

Bibliometric trends can document how the published record is shifting, but they cannot by themselves reveal the human incentives driving those patterns. To understand why researchers are adopting AI so rapidly—and how journal professionals perceive competitive pressures, peer-review standards, and realistic safeguards—Part II examines parallel pilot surveys collected at both the starting point and endpoint of the scientific publication chain.
