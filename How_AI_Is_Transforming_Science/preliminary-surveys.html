How AI Is Reshaping Research and Publishing: Perspectives from Researchers and Editors

Complementary pilot surveys representing the starting point and endpoint of the scientific publication chain—41 researchers across 10 self-reported disciplinary categories and 19 journal professionals across 9 disciplines—illuminate rapid AI adoption, dual-sided competitive pressures, an editorial acceptance paradox, and feasible publishing safeguards.

The Researcher Perspective: Rapid Adoption and Drivers

At the starting point of the publication chain, AI adoption across the pilot sample of 41 researchers spanning 10 self-reported disciplinary categories is already widespread: 90.2% (37 of 41) were using or trying AI-based methods, divided evenly between routine use (18/41, 43.9%) and early-stage use (18/41, 43.9%), alongside one respondent just starting (2.4%). Even among the four respondents with no prior use (9.8%), three (75.0%) rated their interest in adoption at four out of five. Nearly half of all respondents (46.3%, 19/41) estimated that at least half of the research groups in their fields currently use AI (Fig. 1a,b).

Among the 37 users and experimenters, falling technical friction stood out as the leading catalyst: 81.1% (30/37) rated lower analytical barriers via generative AI at four or five out of five. Substantive research motivations followed closely—tackling complex, high-dimensional problems (70.3%), fostering interdisciplinary collaboration (59.5%), and gaining new insights and mechanistic understanding (56.8%)—alongside practical infrastructure and efficiency tailwinds such as public data availability (48.6%), access to high-performance computing (48.6%), lower research costs (45.9%), and faster publication cycles (43.2%). Overt external incentives, including perceptions that high-impact journals (29.7%) or formal research evaluation (16.2%) favour AI, ranked lowest (Fig. 1c).

The Publisher & Editor Perspective: Dual-Sided Pressures, Acceptance Paradox, and Quality Concerns

Comparing researchers and journal professionals reveals the same competitive pressure felt from opposite ends of the publication chain. Among researchers, 78.0% (32/41) expected peers who do not adopt AI to face a disadvantage in academic competition. Among the 19 journal professionals across nine disciplines, 78.9% perceived at least moderate changes in research paradigms associated with AI-augmented research, and 84.2% (16/19) perceived a publication disadvantage for non-AI studies (Fig. 2a).

Yet editorial gatekeeping reveals a clear acceptance paradox: only two of the 19 editors (10.5%) considered AI-augmented manuscripts more likely to receive final acceptance. Core scientific evaluation gates remained decisive, with reproducibility and genuine scientific contribution each rated very important or essential by 78.9% of journal professionals. Meanwhile, although majorities of both researchers (58.5%) and editors (52.6%) expected positive effects on long-term disciplinary sustainability—alongside notable neutral (24.4% / 26.3%) and unsure (14.6% / 15.8%) shares, and very few outright negatives (2.4% / 5.3%; Fig. 2b)—respondents consistently warned of three structural scientific quality risks.

Governance at the Publishing Endpoint: Feasibility of Editorial Safeguards

Evaluating ten candidate safeguards highlights a clear divide between transparency verification and active portfolio intervention (Fig. 3). Under the strict feasibility threshold (F+: feasible or highly feasible), disclosure norms ranked first: 63.2% (12/19) for data, code, and model disclosure, and 57.9% (11/19) for standardised AI-use disclosure. Evidence-informed policy revision, external or empirical validation, and reproducibility checks each drew 52.6% F+ (10/19), with reproducibility checks earning the broadest at-least-moderately-feasible (M+) consensus at 89.5% (17/19).

Monitoring journal-level AI use and structural imbalances received more qualified support: 84.2% M+ (16/19), including 42.1% F+ (8/19). By contrast, measures that require editorial offices to actively rebalance research streams ranked lowest—31.6% F+ for supporting underrepresented research and 21.1% F+ for balancing AI and non-AI research—and attracted the largest shares of “not sure” responses (up to 5 of 19 editors). While journals appear positioned to enforce disclosure and verification standards, broader field-level balance likely depends on shared provenance metrics and upstream institutional incentives.

PART II · METHODOLOGICAL NOTES
  • Exploratory convenience samples: Both surveys (41 researchers across 10 self-reported categories; 19 journal editors across 9 disciplines) are small, network-drawn pilots intended to identify directional signals rather than representative population estimates.
  • Distinct competition contexts: Researchers evaluated disadvantages in academic competition, whereas editors evaluated disadvantages in publication competition.
  • Feasibility thresholds (F+ / M+): F+ sums Feasible and Highly feasible; M+ adds Moderately feasible. Both use all 19 editors as denominator, including “not sure” responses.
